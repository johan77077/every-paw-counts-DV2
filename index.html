01 · Who rescues

Australia's rescue network

When a pet is lost, dumped or surrendered, it is often a small local charity that steps in. Australia has 611 registered pet rescue charities, from big-city shelters to a handful of volunteers fostering animals in their own homes.

They cluster where people live, but they also reach far beyond the cities, into country towns right across Australia.

Hover over a dot to see the group's name. Shows 490 of the 611 charities: the rest have no postcode on the ACNC register. Groups sharing a postcode are spread out slightly so each one is visible. Wildlife carers are not included.

Most of these groups are tiny. Nine in ten are classed as small charities, and the typical one runs on about $73,000 a year and 15 volunteers. A handful of big names, such as the RSPCA, the Lost Dogs' Home and Lort Smith, hold three-quarters of all the money.

But money is only half the picture. Switch the chart below to volunteers and the small groups grow: they hold 40% of the people. Australia's pet rescue depends on both: big shelters with the funds, and small groups with the hands.

02 · The people

Powered by volunteers

Between them, Australia's pet rescue charities have 39,298 volunteers and just 3,978 paid staff: about 10 volunteers for every paid worker.

Four in five have no paid staff at all. They run on people giving their time, and on the public's money: donations make up 53% of their income, and government grants just 7%.

Western Australia leans on volunteers the most, with 26 for every paid worker. In the ACT it is just over 3.

03 · The animals

Which animals they rescue

The RSPCA, Australia's largest animal welfare organisation, took in 77,464 animals in 2024–25. That is a quarter fewer than five years earlier.

Almost all of the drop is cats and dogs: 40% fewer cats and 29% fewer dogs arrived. Wildlife and other animals have barely changed, and now make up nearly half of everything the RSPCA cares for.

Hover over a point for the exact numbers. "Wildlife and other animals" includes horses, livestock, birds, rabbits and other small pets.

Every pet that is desexed, microchipped and kept safely at home is one less animal that needs rescuing.

Each kind of animal leaves care in a different way. Being adopted is the most common outcome for dogs and cats, and one in five dogs goes back home to its owner. Horses, farm animals and small pets like rabbits are more likely to be still in care at the end of the year, waiting for the right home.

Wildlife is the RSPCA's single biggest group, at 27,794 animals. Most arrive sick or badly injured, so two in three are euthanised. One in five is passed on to other groups, such as specialist wildlife carers.

04 · Working together

How rescue organisations work together

Victoria publishes what happened to every dog and cat that passed through its shelters and pounds. In 2025, its 68 council pounds, animal welfare shelters and volunteer rescue groups took in 75,440 dogs and cats.

Two in five of those animals went to just five places, led by the Lost Dogs' Home in North Melbourne. Most of the others are small council pounds in country towns.

Each type of organisation plays a different part. Council pounds hold lost animals so their owners can find them. They pass on a third of all their animals (11,777 in 2025) to shelters and rescue groups that can rehome them.

Volunteer rescue groups are often the last stop. They find new homes for 85% of the animals in their care, and euthanase just 5%.

How do small groups do it? Foster carers. Volunteer rescue groups place 61 of every 100 animals they take in with a foster family, compared with just 3 in 100 at council pounds.

A foster home gives an animal time to settle, lets carers learn its personality, and frees up a pen for the next one. It is one of the easiest ways to help without adopting.

05 · Responsible ownership

Dogs go home, cats don't

What happens to an animal depends a lot on whether it is a dog or a cat.

Nearly half of all dogs go straight back home, often because they are microchipped or registered and their owner can be found. Only 7 in 100 cats are ever reclaimed. For cats, adoption is the main way out, and 28 in 100 are euthanised.

Each square is one in every 100 animals that left a Victorian shelter or pound in 2025. Hover over a square for the exact numbers.

Where a cat ends up matters too. In much of Melbourne, most cats leave alive. In seven country councils, mostly in the north and west, fewer than half do. In Strathbogie, just 8% did.

Dogs fare well almost everywhere: switch the map to dogs and nearly every council turns dark.

Switch between cats and dogs under the map. Hover over a council for its numbers. "Left alive" means reclaimed by an owner, adopted, or passed on to another group. Figures are counted where the shelter is, not where the animal came from. Grey councils have no shelter of their own, or handled fewer than 30 animals.

Why are so many cats euthanised? Half of them had no owner who could be found: they were unidentified, wild or feral.

This is where responsible ownership matters most. A microchipped, desexed cat kept safely at home is far less likely to end up in a pound, and far more likely to get home if it does.

06 · Your part

How you can help

Adopting is a lifelong promise. In 2025, more than 1,000 animals adopted from Victorian shelters were brought back within six months, most often because of their behaviour.

Shelters can help match the right pet to the right home, so ask plenty of questions before you adopt.

And if you can't adopt, you can still help

Microchip and desex

The surest way to get a lost pet home, and to keep unwanted litters out of pounds.

Foster

Rescue groups foster 61 in every 100 animals. A spare room can save a life.

Volunteer

Four in five pet rescue charities have no paid staff. They run on people like you.

Donate

Donations are over half of rescue groups' income. Government gives just 7%.
